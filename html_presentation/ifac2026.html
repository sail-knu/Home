First, let me briefly explain the background. An autonomous surface vehicle, or ASV, is basically a ship that can navigate and control itself without direct human operation.

Just like autonomous cars have to follow traffic rules, autonomous ships also have to follow maritime traffic rules. These rules are called COLREGs, the international rules for preventing collisions at sea.

COLREGs specify how ships should maneuver when they encounter other ships, for example in overtaking, head-on, and crossing situations, as shown here.

So, an autonomous ship should not only avoid collisions, but also avoid them in the right way according to COLREGs.

Next, let me briefly introduce two representative collision avoidance methods.

First, the velocity obstacle, or VO, method limits the ship’s velocity to avoid potential collisions. It is computationally efficient and can be combined with COLREGs. However, it can sometimes result in overly conservative behavior.

Another widely used approach is model predictive control, or MPC. MPC predicts the future motion of the ships and optimizes the control inputs while considering safety constraints. It generally provides better performance than VO, but it requires much more computation.

So, our research objective is to achieve MPC-level collision avoidance performance with much lower computational cost, while also satisfying COLREGs.

Introduction · Control barrier function (CBF)

A CBF keeps the system inside a safe set

CBF

Safe set and CBF constraint

Pre-defined safe set\(h(\mathbf{x})\ge 0\)
CBF constraint
\(\dot h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})+\gamma\,h(\mathbf{x})\ge 0\)

for some \(\mathbf{u}\in U\), with \(\gamma>0\): \(h\) may decrease, but never faster than the exponential bound \(\dot h=-\gamma h\), so it never crosses zero.

\(\dot h=-\gamma h\) \(\dot h\ge-\gamma h\) Always stays positive: safe
Collision avoidance

Distance-based CBF

The CBF limits the speed toward an obstacle in proportion to the relative distance from it: \(-\dot h(\mathbf{x})\le\gamma\,h(\mathbf{x})\)

Source: Ames et al., “Control barrier functions: Theory and applications,” ECC, 2019 04

Now let me explain our main tool, the control barrier function, or CBF. The idea of a CBF is simple. We define a safety function, h of x. When h is zero or above, the ship is safe.

The CBF keeps h from going below zero, at all times. So the ship never enters the unsafe region. How is this used for collision avoidance problem?

Please look at the figures with the obstacle. A common choice is h equals distance minus safety radius. Here, the distance is measured to the obstacle. With this CBF, the controller limits the speed toward the obstacle. Far away, the ship can move fast. Close by, it must slow down.

This represents the physical meaning of the CBF constraints.

Challenge

How to integrate COLREGs into CBFs to ensure rule compliance and improve performance?

1
Conventional CBFs lack explicit logic for avoidance direction

In COLREGs the direction is the key point: the rules tell the ship which way to go

2
They do not properly account for ships’ turning limitations

A ship cannot turn in place

Sources: Ames et al., ECC, 2019 (CBF-QP) · Thyri et al., CCTA, 2020; Thyri & Breivik, Field Robotics, 2022 (ship domain + CBF) 05

Then, we can use these constraints to make a safety filter, as shown here. This framework is called CBF-QP. QP stands for quadratic programming.

Any controller gives a desired control input. We call it the nominal input. The QP changes this input as little as possible, so the CBF condition stays true. It works with any controller, and it is very fast.

So, why not use it directly for ships? There are two problems.

First, standard CBFs have no logic for the avoidance direction. But in COLREGs, direction is the key point. The rules tell the ship which way to go.

Second, standard CBFs do not consider the turning limits of a ship. A ship cannot turn in place.

These two problems are the starting point of our work.

Proposed method · Turning circle-based CBF (TC-CBF)

Keep the turning circles free of obstacles

Two TC-CBFs ensure safety by keeping the vehicle’s left and right turning circles, defined by its speed, heading, and turning limit, free of obstacles.

\[R=\alpha\,\frac{u}{r_{\max}}\]

Turning radius at the maximum turning rate \(r_{\max}\); the scaling factor \(\alpha\) enlarges the circle for a smoother turn

\[h_{tr}(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{(x_{tr}-o_x)^2+(y_{tr}-o_y)^2}-(o_r+R+R_s)\]RTC-CBF
\[h_{tl}(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{(x_{tl}-o_x)^2+(y_{tl}-o_y)^2}-(o_r+R+R_s)\]LTC-CBF
  • \(h_{tr}\ge 0\): the right turning circle is clear, so the ship can always escape by turning right
  • \(h_{tl}\ge 0\): the left turning circle is clear, so the ship can always escape by turning left
TC-CBF was introduced within MPC (Lee, Park & Kim, IEEE TCST, 2026); here the two CBFs are used separately to encode the avoidance side 06

Now, here is our simple geometric idea. We call it the turning circle-based CBF, or TC-CBF.

Imagine a ship moving forward. If it turns as hard as it can, to the left or to the right, it follows a circle on that side. We call these the left and right turning circles.

Please look at the figure. The radius R depends on the current speed and the maximum turning rate. R equals alpha times the speed u, divided by r max. alpha is a scaling factor. A larger alpha makes the circle bigger and the turn smoother.

Now, the safety idea is very simple. We just keep the turning circle away from the obstacle. As shown in the figure, we measure the distance from the center of the turning circle to the obstacle. This distance should be larger than R plus the safety radius.

From this, we define two CBFs: one for the right turning circle, called the RTC-CBF, and one for the left turning circle, called the LTC-CBF. If the right turning circle is clear, the ship has a safe way to escape by turning right. If the left circle is clear, it can escape by turning left.

Now, let me show you the overall framework. First, the LOS guidance and PD controller generate the nominal control input based on the waypoint path.

At the same time, the decision-making module identifies the encounter situation based on the COLREGs rules. Then, for each traffic ship, it decides whether to use the LTC-CBF or the RTC-CBF.

These selected CBF constraints are sent to the safety filter. The safety filter modifies the nominal control input only when necessary, and generates the safe control input for the ASV.

So, the main idea is simple: the decision-making module chooses the avoidance side, and the TC-CBF guarantees safety on that side.

Next, we tune the two main parameters of the TC-CBF.

On the left, we change alpha. A larger alpha gives a larger turning circle, resulting in earlier and smoother avoidance.

On the right, we change gamma. A smaller gamma makes the safety constraint more conservative and increases the avoidance distance.

Based on these results, we use alpha equals 5 and gamma equals 0.02 in the following simulations.
